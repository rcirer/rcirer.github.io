The force–length curve, and where each muscle lives on it

The curve1,2 is the same for both muscles: it is a property of the sarcomere, not of the muscle. What differs is which stretch of it each one travels. Every muscle has two bands: the thin one is everything the joints allow, the thick one is what the exercise covers with the selected range, and the vertical marks are its ends. The grey zones are not range the exercise leaves out: they are lengths the knee and hip cannot reach by any means.

The bands are drawn one above and the other below the curve on purpose: the rectus femoris stretch falls inside the vasti one, and overlaid they would be indistinguishable. The offset is purely visual — the correct height is the curve's, and each muscle's dot does sit on it, joined to its band by a line.

This chart is, in fact, the most important one on the page. Three studies measure vastus lateralis at the same angles and return answers that do not overlap: ascending limb, optimal range or descending limb depending on whom you believe. And vastus medialis measured intraoperatively11 reaches 4.1 µm at 90° of flexion, which is next to zero active force — the authors themselves call these values "dramatically longer" than the biomechanical model predicts. What it would look like on an electromyograph

Three channels on the same angle axis as the charts above. The signal only exists inside the trained range, because outside it the muscle is not contracting. The pale line on each channel is the envelope, which is what an electromyograph gives you after rectifying and smoothing.

This is a simulation, not a recording. The fine trace is generated noise, not a signal measured in anyone. What is grounded is the amplitude: how it splits between the three muscles and how it responds to the hip comes from the literature. Use it to see the pattern; do not take numbers from it.

And the pattern is slightly uncomfortable, because EMG does not follow the force–length story. Kukić and colleagues12 measured vastus lateralis and vastus medialis during maximal contraction at six knee angles and normalised EMG did not change significantly, even though moment did change and peaked. So activation is not a readout of capacity: here amplitude varies because the relative effort the load demands varies with angle, not because the muscle "activates more" at a particular length.

What does differ markedly between muscles is rectus femoris. At maximal contraction it produces lower-amplitude signals than the vasti, and above all: lying supine, its normalised EMG drops by 41 %, against 20 % for the vasti9. Move the hip control and you will see it. It is the same asymmetry that showed up on the force–length curve, measured by a completely different route.

Vastus intermedius is absent because it cannot be recorded with surface electrodes: it is deep. With intramuscular electrodes, Akima and colleagues13 find that it does change with angle — larger amplitude in flexion, decreasing towards extension — and it is precisely the head this page takes its sarcomere lengths from.

Why the three channels look so alike. Because that is what the data says, and it is worth saying so rather than drawing a difference that has not been demonstrated. Looking for a distinct shape for rectus femoris, what you find is this: the published difference from the vasti is mostly one of amplitude and above all of hip sensitivity, not of pattern across the knee's range. The only report of a distinct pattern is Duffell and colleagues'14 — rectus femoris more active than vastus lateralis and vastus medialis obliquus in the final degrees of extension — and it is fragile: it appears only at 30°/s, disappears at higher velocities and is absent isometrically. I have not put it into the model. The head that does have a clearly distinct angle pattern is vastus intermedius, and it is the one you cannot record from outside.

What the model does do: each channel is divided by its own muscle's relative capacity at that angle, because the further a muscle is from its optimum, the more activation it needs to contribute its share. That gives rectus femoris a shape of its own, because it is biarticular and its length curve is not the vasti's. The effect is real but modest. If you want to see rectus femoris genuinely behave differently, the lever is the hip, not the knee.

How to read it

The curves are not the same thing and the peak does not fall in the same place on each. Demand is the moment the external resistance imposes on the knee. Capacity is the moment the quadriceps can generate at that length. Relative demand is the ratio between the two, and it is the only one that says where it actually gets hard: the sticking point.

Where capacity comes from

From the sarcomere force–length curve1, not from a shape picked by eye. The curve has four segments and is not symmetrical: an ascending limb with a slope of 0.57 per µm, an optimal range running from 2.64 to 2.81 µm, and a descending limb with a slope of 0.70, steeper than the ascending one. Over-lengthening costs more than shortening. And the optimum is a region, not a single point: those two values are the sliding-filament model's prediction for humans, and Gollapudi and Lin2 tested them experimentally in human type-I fibres.

The curve is the same for both muscles, because it is a property of the sarcomere. What differs is which stretch of it each one travels. The vasti are monoarticular and their length depends only on the knee. Rectus femoris also crosses the hip: seated, hip flexion has already shortened it before you start. Move the hip control and watch the purple band slide right while the blue one does not move at all.

The lengths the model uses are active, not resting. The distinction matters: during maximal contraction the tendon stretches and the fibres shorten, so the muscle works lower down the curve than a measurement on a relaxed muscle suggests. The vasti are anchored to the active interval of 1.97 to 2.48 µm between 0° and 90° of knee3; the slope that follows, 0.0057 µm per degree, matches the 0.0055 µm/° Chen and colleagues measured by microendoscopy on vastus lateralis4, with a completely different method.

Something the literature contradicts

That a muscle is weak at both ends of its range and strong in the middle is true of the curve. But that does not mean a given muscle travels the whole curve, and the quadriceps is an extreme case: with everything the joints allow — knee from 0° to 140°, hip from 15° of extension to 120° of flexion — the two muscles together only reach from 1.97 to 2.76 µm. That is barely 27 per cent of the curve. The rest is not range the exercise leaves out: it is range the body cannot reach by any means.

And within that 27 %, active force never drops below 62 %. So the "short" end where a muscle is very weak does not exist at the knee: the most shortened position the quadriceps ever reaches still leaves it almost two thirds of its force. Nor does the other end: the maximum reached, at 140° of flexion, is exactly the entrance to the optimal range.

The consequence is that in this exercise there is no "long" end where the muscle becomes weak again. The more knee flexion, the more capable both are. The sticking point comes out at full extension for two reasons that pull the same way: there the muscle is at the shortest it ever gets, and there the external moment arm is largest.

Lying supine, rectus femoris approaches the optimal range but still does not pass through it. Putting it on the descending limb would take something else: hip extension with the knee deeply flexed, which is what a stretched hamstring position does, not a knee extension machine.

On the moment chart, though, almost none of this is visible, and it is worth understanding why: rectus femoris contributes at most about 45 N·m on an axis that runs to 280, and its whole capacity range is about 25 N·m. The physics is there; the scale eats it. That is why the chart has a second scale button, which divides each muscle by its own maximum. There you see it whole: with the hip strongly flexed, rectus femoris climbs from 44 % to 96 % across the range, and with the hip extended it passes through its optimum around 21° and comes back down to 83 %. The vasti curve, meanwhile, does not move a pixel, because they do not cross the hip.

The practical consequence, which is not obvious on a seated extension machine: the backrest position does not make the exercise appreciably easier or harder, but it does decide which part of its range rectus femoris works in. If the goal is to load it near its optimum, seated at 90° never achieves that.

With a weight, the external moment is m · g · L · cos φ, and cos φ is literally the horizontal distance the figure draws between the knee and the load's line of action. It is largest with the knee extended and it is exactly zero with the shank hanging at 90°, when the load passes right through the axis. That is why seated knee extension is hard at the top: there demand is maximal and quadriceps capacity has already fallen.

The band behaves differently, and the figure shows why: because it is anchored behind the knee, it keeps a moment arm with the shank hanging, exactly where the weight has none. Its profile peaks around 15° of flexion and falls more gently. This is the whole point of variable resistance5: it does not make the exercise harder, it redistributes where the hardness sits.

Try setting the load to 100 % and switching ranges. The partial that works at long muscle lengths lets you lift far more, because it excludes the expensive angle; the partial that works at short lengths lets you lift no more at all, because it keeps it. The practical consequence is that a percentage of 1RM is not comparable across different ranges: the same 70 % means very different absolute loads depending on which angles are included. This is the mechanism behind the result of McMahon and colleagues7, where a long-length partial at 55 % of 1RM matches the full range at 80 %.

On what training at long lengths actually buys you, it is worth not overstating things. The long-length partial beats the short-length one at the gastrocnemius, and by a wide margin (+15.2 % against +3.4 %)6. But with Bayesian analysis at the elbow flexors the differences are trivial8. Long lengths matter, the effect is modest, and it depends on the muscle.

What is exact in the figure and what is simplified

Exact, and drawn to scale:

  • The attachments: rectus femoris from the anterior inferior iliac spine, the vasti from the femoral shaft, both into the quadriceps tendon, patella and patellar tendon down to the tibial tuberosity.
  • The moment arm of the external resistance, measured as the perpendicular distance from the knee axis to the line of action. It is the figure shown in centimetres and it is the one that enters the calculation.
  • The patella as a pulley: the tendon wraps the femoral trochlea, and the shortening of the quadriceps is the arc length it travels over it. That is where the 6.4 cm of excursion between 90° and 0° comes from.
  • The length of the patellar tendon, which stays constant: the patella follows the tibia.

Simplified, and worth knowing:

  • The knee axis is drawn fixed. In reality the condyles roll and glide, and the instantaneous axis shifts during the movement.
  • The quadriceps moment arm is drawn constant (the trochlear radius, about 4 cm). The real one varies with angle, peaking around 20–30° of flexion.
  • The vasti are drawn as a single belly. They are three muscles with different fibre orientations.
  • The hip control moves the backrest, the trunk, the pelvis and the origin of rectus femoris. It does not touch the external resistance, and it is correct that it does not: the thigh stays resting on the seat and the shank still rotates in the same plane relative to gravity. That said, below about 60° of hip flexion you are no longer on a normal knee extension machine, you are lying down.
  • The band anchor is a choice, not a datum: 45 cm below the knee axis and 25 cm behind. Moving it changes the profile, and by a lot. Anchored directly below the knee, the band would lose its moment arm in flexion and become an exaggerated version of the weight instead of complementing it. It is exactly the kind of detail that decides whether a band is any use in a given exercise.

What is solid and what is contested

Solid, and published: the shape of the force–length curve and its human parameters1,2; that vastus lateralis sarcomere lengths change by about 0.0055 µm per degree of knee4; and that rectus femoris is about 10 % longer supine than seated9.

Contested, and worth knowing:

  • Where exactly the quadriceps lives on the curve is unresolved. Chen and colleagues4 measure 2.84 µm at 50° and 3.17 µm at 110° — optimal range and then descending limb; Ando and colleagues3 measure 2.19–2.46 µm between 0° and 90°, on the ascending limb throughout. Different methods, opposite pictures. This version uses active lengths, which puts it on Ando's side. With Chen's resting lengths the picture changes completely and the muscle would indeed move onto the descending limb.
  • The starting point for rectus femoris (2.10 µm) comes from no source: it is a choice of mine, anchored to the 10 % difference between supine and seated and split between hip and knee by the ratio of moment arms.
  • The split of moment between the vasti and rectus femoris (205 and 45 N·m) comes from physiological cross-sectional area, but it is approximate.
A discrepancy the model does not paper over. With these numbers capacity rises all the way to 90° of flexion, whereas extensor moment measured in people peaks around 60–70° and then falls slightly. The difference comes from two things absent here: the quadriceps moment arm, constant in this model and in reality varying with angle, and hamstring co-activation. There is also a counter-intuitive published result: maximal voluntary contraction is 10.6 % greater seated than supine9, even though supine puts rectus femoris closer to its optimum. It is attributed to neural activation — normalised rectus femoris EMG drops by 41 % supine — which is something this model does not contain.
Inertial resistance is absent, and deliberately so. A weight or a band has a profile that is a function of angle and can be drawn on these axes. A flywheel depends on acceleration, not position: it would need a model of the movement in time, and load is not quantified the same way10.
