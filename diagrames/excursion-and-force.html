The force–length curve

The curve1 is a property of the sarcomere, the same for both muscles; what differs is which stretch of it each one travels. It has four segments and is not symmetrical: ascending limb of slope 0.57 per µm, optimal range from 2.64 to 2.81 µm, descending limb of slope 0.70. Over-lengthening costs more than shortening, and the optimum is a region, not a point — the sliding-filament prediction, tested experimentally in human type-I fibres2.

Each muscle has two bands: thin for everything the joints allow, thick for what the exercise covers. They are drawn one above and one below the curve because the rectus femoris stretch falls inside the vasti one — the offset is visual only, the correct height is the curve's. The grey zones are lengths the knee and hip cannot reach at all.

The lengths are active, not resting: during maximal contraction the tendon stretches and the fibres shorten, so the muscle sits lower on the curve than a relaxed measurement suggests. The vasti are anchored to 1.97–2.48 µm between 0° and 90° of knee3; the resulting 0.0057 µm per degree matches the 0.0055 µm/° measured by microendoscopy with a completely different method4.

A muscle does not travel the whole curve. With everything the joints allow (knee 0–140°, hip from 15° of extension to 120° of flexion) the two muscles together only reach 1.97 to 2.76 µm: barely 27 % of it. And within that 27 %, active force never drops below 62 %. So neither extreme exists at the knee — the most shortened position the quadriceps ever reaches still leaves it two thirds of its force, and the longest it reaches is merely the entrance to the optimal range.

Where each muscle works

Same axis as the curve, so you can drop your eye from one to the other. One source per muscle, chosen as the one that best shows the phenomenon. Every study measured at two knee angles; from that slope the bars are recomputed for the range you select, and the dot marks the angle you have selected. The solid part is what was actually measured, the pale part is extrapolation.

Read the differences between the bars with care. Three muscles, three methods: vastus lateralis by in vivo microendoscopy4, vastus medialis intraoperatively11, vastus intermedius by ultrasound and modelling3. The gaps between bars are therefore not cleanly differences between muscles — method is mixed in. The only internally valid muscle-to-muscle comparison in this literature is vastus lateralis against vastus medialis within Son's study11, the one work that measured two heads with the same protocol.
And the disagreement is larger than the chart shows. Three studies have measured vastus lateralis at comparable angles and returned answers that do not overlap: 2.28–2.30 µm3, 2.84–3.17 µm4 and 3.20–3.80 µm11 — ascending limb, optimal range or descending limb depending on whom you believe. This page shows the middle one, as the most direct measurement in awake humans.

Knee moment

Demand is the moment the external resistance imposes on the knee. Capacity is the moment the quadriceps can generate at that length. With a weight the external moment is m · g · L · cos φ — literally the horizontal distance the figure draws — so it is largest with the knee extended and exactly zero at 90°, when the load passes through the axis. The band is anchored behind the knee, so it keeps a moment arm where the weight has none: it does not make the exercise harder, it redistributes where the hardness sits, which is the point of variable resistance5.

Set the load to 100 % and switch ranges. The long-length partial lets you lift far more, because it excludes the expensive angle; the short-length one lets you lift no more, because it keeps it. So a percentage of 1RM is not comparable across ranges — the mechanism behind McMahon's result, where a long-length partial at 55 % of 1RM matches the full range at 80 %7. Worth not overstating it: +15.2 % against +3.4 % at the gastrocnemius6, but trivial differences at the elbow flexors under Bayesian analysis8.

Hip position does not make the exercise appreciably easier or harder, but it decides which part of its range rectus femoris works in. On this chart that is nearly invisible — rectus femoris contributes at most 45 N·m on an axis running to 280 — which is what the % of own max button is for. There, with the hip strongly flexed it climbs from 44 % to 96 % across the range; with the hip extended it passes through its optimum around 21° and comes back down to 83 %. The vasti curve does not move a pixel, because they do not cross the hip.

Relative demand

Demand divided by capacity, angle by angle. It is the only curve that says where the lift actually gets hard: its peak is the sticking point, and above 100 % the lift fails at that angle. Everything else on this page is input to it.

In a seated knee extension the sticking point lands at full extension, and it does so because two things pull the same way: there the quadriceps is at the shortest it ever gets, and there the external moment arm is largest — 40 cm against 0 at 90°. Of the two, the moment arm does most of the work: the muscle only loses about a third of its force across the whole range, while the moment arm goes from everything to nothing.

Electromyography

Three channels on the same angle axis. The signal only exists inside the trained range. The pale line is the envelope: what an electromyograph gives you after rectifying and smoothing.

This is a simulation, not a recording. The fine trace is generated noise. What is grounded is the amplitude — how it splits between muscles and how it responds to the hip. Use it to see the pattern; do not take numbers from it.

The pattern is uncomfortable, because EMG does not follow the force–length story. Kukić and colleagues12 measured vastus lateralis and vastus medialis at six knee angles during maximal contraction and normalised EMG did not change significantly, even though moment did. Activation is not a readout of capacity: amplitude here varies with the relative effort the load demands, not with muscle length.

The three channels look alike because that is what the data says. The published difference between rectus femoris and the vasti is one of amplitude and, above all, of hip sensitivity: supine, its normalised EMG drops 41 % against 20 % for the vasti9. The lever is the hip, not the knee. The only report of a distinct angle pattern (Duffell14) appears at 30°/s only and is absent isometrically, so it is not in the model. The head that genuinely does have its own pattern is vastus intermedius13 — and it is too deep to record from the surface.

What is exact, what is simplified, what is contested

Exact and drawn to scale: the attachments; the external moment arm, measured as the perpendicular distance from the knee axis to the line of action, which is the figure that enters the calculation; the patella as a pulley, from which the 6.4 cm of excursion between 90° and 0° follows; and the constant patellar tendon length.

Simplified:

  • The knee axis is fixed; really the condyles roll and glide.
  • The quadriceps moment arm is constant (4 cm); really it peaks around 20–30°.
  • The vasti are one belly; really three muscles, and vastus lateralis and medialis share a curve here because no source separates them by a method compatible with the rest of the model.
  • The hip control moves the backrest, trunk, pelvis and rectus femoris origin, but not the external resistance — correctly, since the thigh stays on the seat. Below about 60° of hip flexion you are lying down, not on a machine.
  • The band anchor (45 cm below the knee axis, 25 cm behind) is a choice. Moving it changes the profile a lot: anchored directly below the knee, the band becomes an exaggerated weight instead of a complement.

Contested:

  • Where the quadriceps sits on the curve is unresolved. Chen measures 2.84 µm at 50° and 3.17 µm at 110°4; Ando measures 2.19–2.46 µm between 0° and 90°3. Opposite pictures. This version uses active lengths, which puts it on Ando's side; with Chen's resting lengths the whole picture changes.
  • The rectus femoris starting point (2.10 µm) comes from no source: it is my choice, anchored to the 10 % supine-versus-seated difference9.
  • The 205 / 45 N·m split between the vasti and rectus femoris is approximate.
A discrepancy the model does not paper over. Capacity here rises to 90° of flexion, whereas measured extensor moment peaks around 60–70°. Missing: the varying quadriceps moment arm and hamstring co-activation. And a counter-intuitive published result — maximal voluntary contraction is 10.6 % greater seated than supine9, despite supine putting rectus femoris closer to its optimum. Attributed to neural drive, which this model does not contain.
Inertial resistance is absent, deliberately. A weight or a band has a profile that is a function of angle. A flywheel depends on acceleration, so it would need a model of the movement in time, and load is not quantified the same way10.
